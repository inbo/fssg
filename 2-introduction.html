Evaluation of the Fish Spiral Sluice Gate as a fish migration facility under experimental conditions

2 Introduction

2.1 Context

Migration barriers and habitat fragmentation are the main threats impacting freshwater and diadromous fish species globally (Belletti et al., 2020). Though barrier removal is often the preferred course of action, it is not always feasible because of economical or safety-related reasons. Potential other solutions to promote fish movement include fish passes and fish elevators, though there efficiency is often strongly dependent on the local conditions and target species (Noonan et al., 2012). Therefore these systems need to be thoroughly assessed to understand how the efficiency is determined by the environmental conditions and target species.

2.2 Design of the Fish Spiral Sluice Gate (FSSG)

The Fish Spiral Sluice Gate (FSSG) of Vandezande was designed to provide a simple, compact solution for fish to overcome barriers between 1 m and 10 m high. It consists of three winded immobile “canals” shaped as a vertical Archimedes’ screw, each with an entry (i.e., the lower valves) and exit (i.e., the upper valves). The lower and upper valves are surrounded by an outflow/inflow chamber. Each canal has an artificial substratum from gravel that is glued on the Archimedes’ screw, is completely filled with water and contains five pressure sensors: three throughout the canal, one at the entrance and one at the exit. The lower and upper valves are adjusted accordingly to the pressure inside of the canal. When the lower valve is fully opened then the upper valve opening is at its smallest. The main idea is for fish to be attracted by the flow at the lower valve when it starts to open, enter the system when the lower valve fully opens and make their way towards the upper valve, to then exit the “canal” when the upper valve opens.

2.3 Aims

The aim of this study is to assess the efficiency of the FSSG and understand how fish of different species and sizes respond to its mechanisms. In doing so, recommendations can be formulated on how appropriate and feasible the use of the FSSG for fish passage would be under different conditions. The assessment was done via an experimental set-up under controlled conditions with live fish.

2.4 Experimental set-up

As the FSSG is a relatively complex system, designed to enable safe passage via the subsequent opening of multiple valves, a traditional set-up in which fish are provided at the entrance and recaptured at the exit to determine mortality rate and type of injuries, would be insufficient (Bierschenk et al., 2018). To really understand the system and how it affects migrating fish, there are more questions to be answered than the general question: How many fish make it through the exit? More specifically, questions that need to be answered are: When do fish enter the FSSG, when do they leave it, how much time (or cycles) does it take them to find the entrance and move up the FSSG, how does experience with the system alter fish behaviour and how does the fish size and species determine the passage efficiency?

The unique operating conditions of the FSSG and multiple questions to be answered, required an alternative assessment approach. In 2023 preliminary tests were conducted to test different methodologies. Fyke net experiments and pit telemetry experiments were evaluated and fine-tuned to come up with a sound methodology that was used during multiple experiments of 2024. The fyke net experiments, which involved fish release and recapture, were found insufficient to provide sound insights as only few fish could be recaptured. For this reason, in 2024, fyke nets were replaced with a recapture method that utilises dip nets, offering the fish more space and shelter in the upper basin and inflow compartment. In 2023 multiple fish species were used during the preliminary tests, but to provide enough statistical power to draw sound conclusions it was decided to use one species instead for which sufficient individuals were used. The aim of this set-up was to encourage fish to remain in the upper basin until the end of each experiment. The PIT telemetry experiments on the other hand were found to be very suitable in combination with the new recapture method and after some tests to optimize placement and detection probability, the methodology was fixed.

2.5 PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) telemetry

PIT telemetry is a widely used technology, enabling the tracking of fish or terrestrial animals, especially in narrow systems (Thorstad et al., 2013). Similar to other telemetry techniques, it relies on a tag inserted or fixed on a fish but also on an antenna, which generates a magnetic field, charging the tag which then emits a response that can be read by the system (Bubb et al., 2002). Two types of PIT telemetry exist: Half-Duplex (HDX) and Full-Duplex (FDX). In FDX systems, the antenna continuously generates a magnetic field and the tag response can constantly be detected and recorded. HDX systems switch between the “generating a magnetic field” mode and “reading tag response” mode. Antennae are simpler in HDX, can be larger and also require less power. They are usually made from single or multiple loops of insulated wired electrical cable, generating the magnetic field. This antenna is then connected to a tuning box, which is linked to a reader box where the data logging computer is located (Barbour et al., 2011). The tracked animal is usually tagged internally in the abdominal cavity with a different tag depending on whether the system is HDX or FDX. Tags of different sizes can be used, with the large ones having a strong signal and the smaller ones having a weaker signal. The signal range can be impacted by the presence of metal around the antennae, although it is often still usable. HDX telemetry was used in the present study.
