Evaluation of the Fish Spiral Sluice Gate as a fish migration facility under experimental conditions

4 Discussion

For the experiments exp1 to exp3, the cycle duration and phase distribution can be considered the same, though this was not apparent from the data because of the too low temporal resolution of the exp1 valve-opening data. The lower valve was closed most of the time, while the upper valve was open most of the time. Fish generally wait until the valves are completely open before entering and leaving the FSSG, though for the upper valve a relatively large amount of fish also seem to leave the FSSG when the valve is opening or closing.

Despite operational settings of the FSSG throughout the experiments of 2024 being fixed, entrance efficiency and passage efficiency varied substantially as they ranged from 0.70 to 0.98, and from 0.68 to 0.84, respectively. It is very difficult to determine the underlying cause behind this difference as these values are not exact (especially the entrance efficiency might have been overestimated due to the experimental design and detection range reaching outside the entrance) and local, potentially interfering, conditions might have been present but were only sporadically measured (e.g. water quality) (Bunt et al., 2012). In addition, though the available results did not indicate any effect, the familiarity with the FSSG of fish that were re-used for the different experiments might have had an effect. The length and weight did however not seem to have any effect on whether fish were able to successfully use the FSSG, nor did it have an effect on the time it took fish to move up the FSSG. Only a limited number of fish went through the FSSG multiple times. Some preliminary tests with other species indicated there was no clear difference between species in the passage succes or passage timing.

When assessing fish passage four different aspects should be assessed: attraction efficiency (the proportion of approaching fish that locate the fishway entrance), entrance efficiency (proportion of attracted fish that enter the fishway), passage efficiency (proportion of entered fish that successfully pass the fishway) and overall efficiency (proportion of approach fish that are attracted to, enter, and successfully pass the fishway) (Baker et al., 2019).

Regarding the attraction efficiency, it should be noted that fish were forced into the narrow lower basin with no-where else to go than towards the lower valve. It is reasonable to assume that all fish within this lower basin where in the attraction zone of the FSSG and could all be classified as attracted to the FSSG. Whether the provided attraction flow would be strong enough to direct upstream-migrating riverine fish towards the inlet of the FSSG (i.e., What would be the attraction efficiency?) in a real-life situation, could not be assessed under these fixed laboratory settings and would require field tests on sites that resemble the locations the FSSG is intended for or experiments with larger approach zones. The FSSG therefore has the potential to aid fish in their migration, but placement and sound operational settings will most likely be key. Though the relatively low amount of water that passes through the FSSG is beneficial for water managers that want to prevent too much water to flow from one side of a sluice or hydropower installation to the other, it is not ideal to attract fish and make them move through the system. Both the unnatural low amount and the discontinuous, interrupted supply of water via the single inlet (which could be remediated by the use of all three canals in combination with an additional attraction flow (not tested here)) are likely to limit the ability for fish to find the FSSG entrance (i.e., attraction efficiency), enter it (i.e., entrance efficiency) and move successfully through it (i.e., passage efficiency)(Bunt et al., 2012). Assessments of attraction efficiency in real-life situations are strongly advised.

Despite its high variability, the entrance efficiency in this study was relatively high (0.70 to 0.98). It is however key to mention here that the calculated entrance efficiency is most likely an overestimation from the actual entrance efficiency that was obtained in this lab setting. Few studies have assessed entrance efficiency before but the few studies available describe mainly lower values. More specifically, Arctic grayling, trout and suckers were found to have an entrance efficiency of 0.44 to 0.79 for a Denil fishway (Triano et al., 2022) while barbel and chub were found to have an entrance efficiency of 0.10 to 0.80 for a vertical slot and multislot fishway (Romão et al., 2021). It should be noted however that not only the design of the fishways in these and our study differed but that also the tested ambient conditions and experimental set-up were distinct. In the first study, 14 field trails were conducted at six Denil fishways with varying slopes in the upper Big Hole River basin (Montana, USA) from June to October. Wild local fish were captured via electrofishing and PIT-tags were inserted. A set-up of four antennas was used to determine attraction, entrance and passage efficiency. The second study, consisted of a lab experiment for which a full-scale model was installed inside a flume with glass walls to be able to visually observe fish movement. Wild-caught fish were used and observers visually determined the moments of entry and passage per fish. Average water velocity within the fishway varied between 0.2 and 0.5 m/s. Despite, the high entrance efficiency established in this study being promising, additional monitoring in more real-life situations is advised as there was no way to quantify the level of overestimation tied up with this metric.

Similarly, despite its high variability the passage success was found to be relatively high (0.68-0.84). Unlike the entrance efficiency however, the calculated passage efficiency was most likely an underestimation from the actual passage efficiency that was obtained in this lab setting. Though other fish passage structures are also characterized by a wide range in passage efficiency, on average passage efficiency decreases from nature-like fishways (0.70), to Denil (0.51), vertical-slot (0.45) and pool-and-weir (0.34) fishways (Bunt et al., 2012). The passage efficiency of the FSSG appears to be closest to that of a nature-like fishway which is a very promising outcome. However, the referred to studies of this review were all conducted under natural conditions (Bunt et al., 2012). One lab study on bony herring passing Denil fishways presented a value of 0.88 for a slope of 8 %(Mallen-Cooper & Stuart, 2007), while another lab study reported passage efficiencies on innovative tube fishways between 0.64 and 0.98 (Harris et al., 2020). Though, both field and lab studies are characterized by pronounced variability related to, amongst others, species and fishway type, length and slope (Noonan et al., 2012), lab studies have a tendency to obtain better results than field tests due to the controlled parameters and foreceful nature of the former (Sun et al., 2023). A postive outcome of lab tests should preceed any attempt to implement a system in rivers or canals. However, even after lab tests provide promising results, an assessment under natural conditions is strongly recommended before considering installation on multiple sites.

Comparing the overall efficiency, which brings together attraction, entrance and passage efficiency, of this study with the results of other studies is not advised as attraction efficiency could not be assessed.

Despite the high efficiencies, entrance time (time from release to entry; most likely an underestimation) and passage time (time from entry to exit) were typically quite high. This was somehow to be expected as approaching fish have to wait for the different valves to open and close. Fish that missed a cycle to hitch along would need to wait for the next. The first 50% of fish that would enter, did so in 100 to 200 minutes while the majority of Arctic grayling, trout and suckers were found to enter a Denil fishway in less than one hour after their release. Passage times are very difficult to compare due to differences in the fishway type, volumetric power dissipation, height drop between pools, slope, obstacle height, pool depth, species and fork length in the fishway (Bravo-Córdoba et al., 2021).

Limitations of the study

During the first experiment of 2024 it seemed that the temporal resolution of the valve data was too low to capture the moment the valves opened and closed. The last two experiments had sound valve measurement data for the entire duration of the experiment at an adequate temporal resolution. Another important limitation, typical for any laboratory experiment, involves the controlled conditions that not necessarily mimic real-life conditions. Not only will actual river conditions be different from those in the lab, river conditions are also known to vary strongly in time and space. Since in this study laboratory conditions were fixed, the effect environmental conditions might have had, could not be assessed. In addition, the cultured fish themselves, sedated, operated and transported, are unlikely to show the same behaviour (e.g., movement, intent and motivation) as wild fish would under natural conditions (Noonan et al., 2012). Finally, the limitations of the experimental design and pit telemetry set-up also prevented the estimation of attraction efficiency and introduced some important uncertainty tied up with the estimates of the other metrics.
