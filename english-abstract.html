Evaluation of the Fish Spiral Sluice Gate as a fish migration facility under experimental conditions

English abstract

Context: Well-functioning fishway passes are needed worldwide to promote fish migration as the number of barriers is unlikely to decrease in the future. The Fish Spiral Sluice Gate (FSSG) was designed to provide a simple, compact solution for fish to overcome barriers between 1 m and 10 m high. It features a multi-chamber valve system with adjustable current options in both the inflow and outflow chambers. The principle is as follows: fish are attracted by the flow at the lower valve when it starts to open, enter the system when the lower valve fully opens and make their way towards the upper valve, to then exit when the upper valve opens.

Aim: Before new designs of fishway passes are introduced, there should be some rigorous testing of the attraction, entrance, passage and overall efficiency. The aim of this report was to assess how well the FSSG functions under experimental conditions and to understand how fish behave before, during and after passage.

Methods: Testing consisted of three experiments with ide (Leuciscus idus). Pit tags were inserted in fish, which allowed to determine when tagged fish passed the lower valve and upper valve as both were equipped with antennas. In addition, the opening and closing of the valves were monitored. The obtained data allowed to determine the different types of fishway efficiency, the time of entrance and passage, the moment of entrance and exit in function of the valve openings, and the effect of length, weight and species on aforementioned aspects.

Results: Despite operational settings of the FSSG throughout the experiments being fixed, entrance efficiency and passage efficiency varied substantially as they ranged from 70 % to 98 %, and from 68 % to 84 %, respectively. Entrance time (time from release to entry) and passage time (time from entry to exit) were relatively high. 50% of all tagged fish had an entrance time of 100 to 200 minutes, while the entered fish had a passage time of 225 to 1200 minutes. The length and weight did not seem to have any effect on whether fish were able to successfully use the fish spiral, nor did it have an effect on the time it took fish to move up the fish spiral. Fish generally wait until the valves are completely open before entering and leaving the FSSG, though for the upper valve a relatively large amount of fish also seem to leave the FSSG when the valve is opening or closing. Some preliminary tests with other species indicated there was no clear difference between species in the passage succes or passage timing.

Implications: Under the experimental conditions tested, entrance and passage efficiencies were relatively high and within the range reported for several other fishway types. Direct comparison with field-tested fishways should, however, be interpreted cautiously because of major differences in experimental conditions. The high variability of both efficiencies is also in line with findings in literature of other fishway designs and highlights the strong effect local conditions and placement could have. However, it should be noted that the calculated entrance and passage efficiency are most likely to be overestimated and underestimated, respectively, from the actual efficiencies that were obtained in this lab setting. It was unfortunately not possible to quantify these levels of uncertainty. Therefore field testing under representative conditions is needed to assess how the FSSG performs in real-life situations. In addition, attraction efficiency could not be tested in this set-up, but might be an important aspect to consider, especially given its strong link with local conditions and placement. As attraction flow can be adapted in the FSSG, it will be important to test out different settings per site. The relatively long transit times were to be expected because the FSSG works in phases and the speed with which a fish can travel is therefore not only determined by its own traits and motivation, but also by the operational settings of the FSSG.

Conclusion: The experiments on the FSSG yielded promising results. However, it should be stressed that the fixed laboratory settings of this study prevented the assessment of the attraction efficiency, a key aspect in fishway design that often turns out to be the limiting factor, and also introduced some serious uncertainty regarding the entrance (overestimation) and passage efficiency (underestimation). Any extrapolation to the wide range of real-life situations should therefore be done with care. Well-advised placement, scientifically sound follow-up and consequent adjustments are key to get the most out of the potential of the FSSG.
